Context

An AI-powered inbox organizer that declutters, categorizes, and surfaces the right information — so you can work faster and smarter.

I built Triage because email was genuinely breaking my brain. Hundreds of unread messages, important things buried under newsletters, and no easy way to find what I actually needed. The inbox — a tool designed to help — had become the thing I dreaded most.

Working with AI

I built this with AI — and learned as much about prompting as I did about design.

I started building in Claude then moved to Cursor. Before writing a single line of code, I gave the AI a persona — and it changed everything.

It didn't just give me better outputs — it helped me think about things I would not have considered before.

Phase-based building

I instructed the AI to work in phases: break down large tasks, ask clarifying questions before moving forward, and never code the whole app at once. This kept me in control of the logic before the UI was built.

Fact-checker sub-agent

To reduce hallucinations, I set up a Playwright MCP sub-agent in Cursor whose only job was to fact-check everything the main agent thought. It acted as the AI's eyes — catching mistakes before they shipped.

Not always smooth sailing.

There were moments where no matter how carefully I phrased a prompt, the AI couldn't understand what I meant. I tried over 6 different variations on one feature. I even took a screenshot of the problem and asked Gemini to write the prompt for me. Nothing worked.

And all I needed was a simple one liner with the term "container height."

Asking the AI "what went wrong?" was often more effective than re-prompting. After one stuck session, the AI added an error boundary message to the screen so I could paste back the exact error — and it figured out what went wrong. I thought that was very smart.

Reflection

Before this I always felt like I was trying to make AI read my mind.

This time it finally felt like AI was my partner instead of a kid I had to teach.

The persona + phase-based approach meant the AI was reasoning with me, not just generating at me. I came away with a working product and a new mental model for how to collaborate with AI that I'll carry into every project from here.

What I'd do differently.

I'd spend more time upfront defining the data schema before touching the UI. Several of my hardest debugging sessions came from UI assumptions that didn't match the underlying data structure. Logic first, interface second.
